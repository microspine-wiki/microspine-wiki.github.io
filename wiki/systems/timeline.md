# Timeline of spine climbers

The table lists systems that attach to rough surfaces with small spines, hooks or claws and for which a paper is listed in the [bibliography](../reference/bibliography.md). It is a map, not a ranking, and it is certainly incomplete: please [add what is missing](../about/contributing.md).

| Year | System | What it showed | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2005 | **Spinybot** (Stanford) | A 0.4 kg robot climbs concrete, brick and stucco on arrays of compliant microspines and holds without power | [Asbeck 2005][asbeck2005]; [Kim 2005][kim2005] |
| 2006–08 | **RiSE** | A 3.8 kg hexapod walks on the ground and climbs brick, stucco and crushed-stone walls with rows of spine toes | [Spenko 2008][spenko2008] |
| 2007 | Human-scale patches (Stanford) | A person hangs from a wall on patches of spine toes | [Asbeck 2007][bdml-outside-testing] |
| 2008 | **Screenbot** (Case Western) | A 126 g robot walks inverted on mesh by pulling opposed spined legs toward each other | [Wile 2008][wile2008] |
| 2009 | **Mini-Whegs** with spines (Case Western) | Spines on simple rotating wheel-legs climb steep concrete | [Daltorio 2009][daltorio2009] |
| 2009 | **RiSE V3** | A 5.4 kg quadruped climbs a wooden pole at 21 cm/s with claws | [Haynes 2009][haynes2009] |
| 2009–11 | **Perching aircraft** (Stanford) | A small fixed-wing aircraft lands on walls with spines and takes off again | [Lussier Desbiens & Cutkosky 2010][desbiens2010]; [Lussier Desbiens 2011a][desbiens2011] |
| 2010 | Design theory | Limit curves, suspension requirements and the stalk model | [Asbeck 2010][asbeck2010]; [Asbeck & Cutkosky 2012][asbeck2012] |
| 2011 | **CLASH** (Berkeley) | A 15 g hexapod climbs loose vertical cloth with spines that engage and release passively | [Birkmeyer 2011][birkmeyer2011] |
| 2011–13 | **Microspine anchors and LEMUR IIB** (JPL) | Radial, opposed arrays grip rock in any orientation; a robot climbs overhangs and drills inverted | [Parness 2011][parness2011]; [Parness 2012][parness2012]; [Parness 2013][parness2013] |
| 2012 | Claw-foot wall climber | Hook-like claws for inspecting rough concrete, with a model of claw and asperity | [Xu 2012][xu2012] |
| 2013–16 | **DROP** and rotary microspines (JPL) | Wheels of compliant spines climb cinder block at 25 cm/s and survive falls | [Parness & McKenzie 2013][parness2013drop]; [Carpenter 2016][carpenter2016] |
| 2015 | **Tbot** | A wheeled robot with rings of spines climbs brick 10° past vertical | [Liu 2015][liu2015] |
| 2015 | **DIGbot** (Case Western) | Distributed inward gripping on vertical and inverted screen | [Palmer 2015][palmer2015] |
| 2016–17 | Dense spine tiles and **RoboSimian** palm (Stanford, JPL) | 19 spines per cm²; a palm holding more than 700 N in shear | [Wang 2016][wang2016]; [Wang 2017][wang2017] |
| 2017 | **SCAMP** (Stanford) | One robot flies, perches, climbs and takes off again outdoors | [Pope 2017][pope2017] |
| 2017 | **LEMUR 3** (JPL) | A four-limbed robot climbs cliffs on microspine grippers and solar panels on gecko adhesive | [Parness 2017a][parness2017] |
| 2017 | Microspine tool for asteroid capture (JPL) | A gripper and anchoring drill baselined for the proposed Asteroid Redirect Mission | [Parness 2017b][parness2017tool] |
| 2018 | Stochastic array models (Stanford) | Predicts force capability of spine arrays from asperity statistics | [Jiang 2018][jiang2018] |
| 2018–21 | Passive spine gripper and **HubRobo** (Tohoku) | A 3 kg quadruped climbs rugged slopes on passive spine grippers | [Nagaoka 2018][nagaoka2018]; [Uno 2021][uno2021] |
| 2019 | **SpinyHand**, soft and spiny paws (Stanford) | Human-scale hands with spine tiles and mechanical load sharing | [Wang 2019][wang2019]; [Ruotolo 2019][ruotolo2019] |
| 2020 | **Nautilus gripper** (JPL) | A 16-finger microspine gripper anchors to rock at 2,100 m depth | [Backus 2020][backus2020] |
| 2020 | **SpinyCrawler** | A tracked robot with opposed spiny grippers climbs rough ceilings | [Liu 2020][liu2020] |
| 2021–25 | **SCALER** (UCLA) | A quadruped free-climbs bouldering walls, overhangs and ceilings | [Tanaka 2022][tanaka2022]; [Tanaka 2025][tanaka2025] |
| 2022 | Printed microspines (Carnegie Mellon) | Toes printed in one flexible material around a fishhook | [Nadan 2022][nadan2022] |
| 2022–24 | **ReachBot** (Stanford) | Spine grippers on long extendable booms, tested in a lava tube | [Chen 2022][chen2022]; [Chen 2024][chen2024] |
| 2024 | **LORIS** (Carnegie Mellon) | A lightweight free-climber with fully passive microspine grippers | [Nadan 2024][nadan2024] |
| 2024 | **MARCBot** | A rock-climbing robot whose spiny grippers also serve as feet | [Zi 2024][zi2024] |
| 2025 | Soft robots with microspine arrays | Passive two-row arrays improve traction of soft crawlers on natural terrain | [Ervin 2025][ervin2025] |
| 2026 | **LIMBERO** (Tohoku) | A 10 kg-class quadruped climbs steep rock with spine grippers | [Uno 2026][uno2026] |

## Three threads

**Walls.** The original problem: flat, hard, vertical building surfaces, with gravity supplying the shear. Spinybot and RiSE, the perching aircraft and the wheeled climbers belong here, and so does the design theory in this wiki.

**Rock, in any orientation.** From 2011 the focus widens to natural rock and to ceilings and overhangs, where gravity no longer helps. The answer is the [opposed array](../design/opposed-spines.md), and it leads to grippers, to limbed free-climbers and to proposals for caves and small bodies in space.

**Scale.** Carrying a robot of tens of kilograms, or a person, needs hundreds to thousands of spines and brings the [alignment problem](../design/arrays-and-patches.md) to the front. The human-scale work at Stanford, the RoboSimian palm and SpinyHand are steps along this thread.
